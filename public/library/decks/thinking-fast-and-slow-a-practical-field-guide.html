01 · TL;DR
The one-minute version

Your mind is efficient, not neutral.

Most of the time, we use quick impressions and familiar patterns. That works brilliantly in everyday life, but it can also turn a guess into a belief before we notice.

Notice

Fast is the default

Intuition generates the first answer: a feeling, story, estimate or impulse.

Test

Slow is a scarce resource

Careful reasoning can correct the first answer, but it needs attention, time and motivation.

Design

Good systems help

Checklists, base rates, defaults and premortems make better thinking easier at the point of choice.

Use the book for:

spotting when a confident story is built from too little evidence, then changing the decision process rather than relying on willpower alone.

02 · BENEFITS
Where it pays off

Better decisions come from better conditions.

Product

Roadmaps and bets

AI / ML

Model and eval choices

Research

Expect noise in small samples. Pre-specify what would change your mind before seeing the result.

Life

03 · CORE MODEL
The core model

Two characters, one mind.

1

System 1 · automatic

  • Fast, associative and effortless
  • Produces impressions, feelings and first answers
  • Excellent with familiar patterns
2

System 2 · effortful

  • Attention-demanding and sequential
  • Handles computation, rules and self-control
  • Can check System 1, but tires easily

These are useful labels for modes of processing, not literal brain modules. The practical question is: what would make a check happen here?

Book · ch. 1–3; Conclusions, “Two Systems”

05 · COGNITIVE EASE
Cognitive ease

Easy to process can feel like true.

Familiarity, repetition, clear presentation and a good mood can make an idea feel more credible or more obvious, even when they are not evidence for the claim.

Useful side

Clear language and legible design reduce needless friction. People can understand the important thing faster.

Risky side

A repeated claim can borrow the feeling of familiarity. A polished demo can borrow the feeling of competence.

Design check:

Make the interface easy to use, but make the evidence hard to fake. Keep provenance, uncertainty and counter-evidence visible.

Book · ch. 5; ch. 7; ch. 10–13
06 · HEURISTICS
The shortcut catalogue

Common shortcuts, translated into plain English.

ShortcutWhat it doesWhere it bites
AvailabilityJudges likelihood by how easily examples come to mind.Recent incidents, vivid demos, news cycles.
RepresentativenessMatches a case to a prototype and neglects base rates.Hiring, diagnosis, user research, “this feels like a winner”.
SubstitutionAnswers an easier question than the one asked.“How good is this strategy?” becomes “Do I like the story?”
AnchoringStarts from a number and adjusts too little.Pricing, estimates, targets and negotiations.

A heuristic is not automatically a mistake. The risk is using a shortcut in a setting where the cues are noisy, the stakes are high or the base rate is ignored.

07 · ANCHORING
Anchoring

The first number can bend the next number.

Kahneman and Tversky found that even arbitrary numbers can pull estimates towards them. People may adjust deliberately, but they often stop too soon.

Low anchorestimate pulled down
Neutral processindependent estimate
High anchorestimate pulled up

Before estimating

Write your independent range first. Separate assumptions from the number already on the table.

When negotiating

Don’t merely counter an extreme anchor. Explain why it is irrelevant and bring evidence from the outside view.

Book · ch. 11, “Anchors”
08 · STATISTICS
Statistical intuition

Small samples produce extreme results more often.

n

The smaller the sample, the more noise can masquerade as a pattern.

Kidney-cancer counties

In the book’s example, both the highest and lowest rates appeared disproportionately in small, rural counties. The shared feature was small sample size, not a rural causal explanation.

Practical implication

Treat a striking result as a prompt to ask: how much data produced this? What happens on replication? What is the comparison group?

Book · ch. 10, “The Law of Small Numbers”; source example is presented as a teaching illustration.
09 · FORECASTING
Forecasting

Confidence follows the story, not necessarily the evidence.

Inside view

“Our plan makes sense.”

Focuses on the particular case: our team, our product, our advantages and our intended path.

Outside view

“What happened to similar cases?”

Starts with a reference class, distribution of outcomes and the obstacles that affected comparable efforts.

1

Make a forecast

Give a range, not just a point estimate.

2

Find the reference class

Use comparable projects, not only your own narrative.

3

Run a premortem

Assume the plan failed and write the history of how.

4

Record the prediction

Review calibration later. Memory edits the past.

10 · PROSPECT THEORY
Prospect theory

Losses usually feel larger than equivalent gains.

The value of an outcome is judged relative to a reference point. As the outcome moves farther away, sensitivity tends to flatten.

Reference point

“Where am I starting?”

A price, current state, expectation or previous outcome can become the baseline that defines gain and loss.

Asymmetry

“How would I feel about losing it?”

The emotional impact of giving something up can outweigh the pleasure of receiving the same thing.

Product implication:

When introducing change, understand the user’s reference point. A “benefit” can still feel like a loss if it removes a familiar capability or control.

Book · ch. 25–29, especially ch. 26 “Prospect Theory”
11 · FRAMING
Framing

Same facts. Different frame. Different choice.

Gain frame

“90% fat-free”

Highlights what remains as a positive attribute.

Loss frame

“10% fat”

Highlights what is given up or at risk.

State the decision

What choice is actually being made?

Flip the frame

Write the same option as gain and loss.

Check invariance

Would a neutral description change your preference?

Choose deliberately

Explain the trade-off in plain language.

Book · ch. 34 “Frames and Reality”; ch. 31 “Risk Policies”
12 · MEMORY
Memory and well-being

The self that experiences is not the self that remembers.

Experiencing self

“How does it feel now?”

Tracks the moment-by-moment experience. Duration matters because moments accumulate.

Remembering self

“How was it overall?”

Builds a compact story, strongly influenced by the peak and the end. Duration can disappear from the summary.

Live experienceMany moments over time
→
MemoryPeak + ending + story
→
Future choiceRepeat, avoid or recommend
Book · ch. 35 “Two Selves”; ch. 36 “Life as a Story”; ch. 37 “Experienced Well-Being”
13 · PEAK-END
Peak-end rule

A good ending can improve the memory of a long experience.

What the experience contains

A whole curve: high and low moments, plus the time spent in each state.

What the memory keeps

A compressed judgement that overweights the most intense moment and the ending.

Before improving an experience, decide which outcome matters: lower total burden, a better memory, or both. They can point to different interventions.

Book · ch. 35; colonoscopy and cold-hand studies described in the source.
14 · LIMITS
Important limits

Use the model as a prompt, not a verdict.

Not literal

System 1 and 2 aren’t brain boxes.

They are shorthand for automatic and effortful operations. The book says this explicitly in its conclusion.

Not universal

Intuition isn’t always a bias.

In stable environments with repeated practice and fast feedback, expert intuition can be valuable.

Not magic

A checklist can still fail.

Good process reduces predictable error; it doesn’t remove uncertainty, incentives or bad data.

Evidence discipline:

Ask what is directly supported by the source, what is a reasonable interpretation, and what is a recommendation for your context.

Book · ch. 22 “Expert Intuition”; Conclusions, “Two Systems” and “Econs and Humans”
Section 03

A decision hygiene protocol

A lightweight routine for slowing down only where slowing down is worth the cost.

Implementation guide · derived from the book’s decision principles.
15 · STEP 01
Step 01 · Before the meeting

Define the decision before discussing the story.

A

Write the question

“Which option should we choose?” not “Do we like option A?”

B

Name the stakes

What is reversible, what is costly and what has a deadline?

C

Set the reference point

What is the baseline, current alternative or default?

D

Record the first estimate

Capture your range before other people’s numbers become anchors.

Implementation recommendation · informed by Book ch. 11, 23, 31 and 34.
16 · STEP 02
Step 02 · During the meeting

Make the hidden shortcuts visible.

Base rate

What happened in comparable cases?

Sample size

How much data supports this pattern?

Alternative story

What else could explain the same evidence?

Frame flip

Would the decision survive a gain/loss rewrite?

Meeting move:

Ask for a “consider-the-opposite” pass before asking for consensus. It is easier to notice a blind spot before the group has publicly committed.

Implementation recommendation · informed by Book ch. 7–18 and ch. 31–34.
17 · STEP 03
Step 03 · Before committing

Use a premortem to release dissent.

Imagine the plan has failed one year from now. Each person has five to ten minutes to write the history of the failure.

Prompt

“It failed because…”

What obstacle, dependency, incentive or assumption did we underestimate?

Output

Risk register, not theatre

Turn each credible failure mode into an owner, an early signal and a mitigation.

PlanIntended path
→
Failure storyCompeting narrative
→
MitigationChange the plan or measure
Book · ch. 24, “The Premortem: A Partial Remedy”
18 · STEP 04
Step 04 · After the decision

Keep a record so confidence can meet reality.

Prediction log

Record the date, forecast, range, assumptions, reference class and confidence.

Outcome: ______
Range: ________
Confidence: ___%
Base rate: _____

Review loop

  1. Compare outcome with forecast.
  2. Separate luck from process quality.
  3. Update the reference class.
  4. Adjust the next estimate.
Don’t rewrite history:

Keep the original prediction. Retrospective coherence is one of the reasons overconfidence survives.

Implementation recommendation · informed by Book ch. 19–24.

20 · FIELD CARD
The one-page field card

When the decision matters, ask these seven questions.

  1. What is the actual question?
  2. What first impression or number is anchoring me?
  3. What is the base rate and how large is the sample?
  4. What evidence would disconfirm my story?
  5. What does the outside view say?
  6. How does the frame change the choice?
  7. Will I later judge the experience by the peak and the end?
Synthesis · Book Parts I–V and Conclusions.
21 · CLOSE
Closing takeaways

Don’t aim to think slowly all the time.

Aim to recognise the moments when a quick answer is expensive, when uncertainty is being hidden, or when the environment is nudging the choice for you.

Slow down selectively

Use effort where the stakes, uncertainty or irreversibility justify it.

Design the process

Base rates, defaults, logs and premortems beat heroic vigilance.

Stay humble

Confidence is a feeling about the story. Calibration is a relationship with outcomes.

Thank you.
